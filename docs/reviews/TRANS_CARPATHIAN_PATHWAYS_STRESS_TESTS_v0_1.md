*Michel's word, October 9, 2026: two or three stress tests. Three
were run the same day, each by a separate agent that had not seen
the white paper written, each in a role chosen to break the design
from a different side: a sceptical funder (money over five years),
a red-team reviewer (adversaries and failures), and an
anthropologist of rural development (capture and co-option). Each
read the white paper v0.1, the proposal v0.2, the co-creator
agreement v0.2, the starting prompt v0.22 and, where relevant, the
reasoning v0.1, the project review v0.1, the manual v0.3 and the
host-fields specification v0.5. Each was told to quote the
documents where it relied on them, to label general knowledge as
such, and to be harsh but fair. Their reports are given in §2 to
§4 as delivered, trimmed only of their reading lists. §1 is what
the three demand in common; §5 is the list of decisions they put
to Michel, each tied to the document it would change. Nothing here
is decided by being written down.*

*One finding was acted on at once, because it was an inconsistency
inside the white paper itself: its §12 asked that corridors be
heard "as evidence … of what the mountains' people want their
mountains to be", which contradicts its §8, "speaks only for
them". White paper v0.2 corrects the line. Every other finding
waits.*

## 1. What the three tests demand in common

Three testers, three vantage points, and the same five things
surfaced from each side. They are listed in the order of how many
tests raised them.

**The Curator is assumed to stand between the map and the door,
and nothing makes it so.** Every safety promise (briefing,
screening, telling the host who is coming, holding a stage, the
permit regime) assumes travelers reach hosts through the Curator.
But host points are public, drawn in their own colour, with
offers, capacity, season and languages on the click, so a traveler
who finds the point and knocks has had none of it, and a scraper
has all of it. The red team ranks this first; the funder's
"everybody comes" scenario shows the Curator bypassed by volume as
well; the anthropologist's Curator's-village scenario shows the
Curator's position has no counterweight when it is not bypassed.
The lever already exists and is unused: the starting prompt §2
records that a Pathways map is a backend copy of Plai whose
security tab grants view "to the public, logged-in users, groups
or single users". *Decision for Michel:* host points visible to
logged-in viewers only during the pilot, or at least a
Curator-contact line on every click.

**One person holds everything, and the promises made in the
agreement are fiction for any six weeks he is unreachable.** All
three tests arrive at A-1 (a legal body) and A-4 (a deputy with
the key and credentials), and the red team adds: the first Curator
must not be the lead, the stewardship mailbox must be read by a
second person, and Mapbender's own database — where every account
lives — is not in the backup. The anthropologist goes further: the
body, not the person, should hold the instance, the marks and the
controller role before the first real account, with Phase 1's end
written into it.

**The money design has one lawful, defined form out of four, and
no cost-carrier after the grant.** The funder walks one traveler's
ten days and finds: "fixed contribution" is a price with a kinder
noun; "ubec-pay or a community currency" cannot be paid with;
exchange of work for a foreigner is unsettled under work-permit
and volunteer law; and payment "through the Curator" (A-3's second
option) would make a tourist product on the paper's own reading of
the law. The only moving part the grant funds — a paid Curator —
is funded before a single traveler exists, with no exit and no
stop rule: the donor-dependent pattern the paper itself cites.
*Decisions:* A-3 closed as direct; OI-P8 revisited for ubec-pay; a
one-paragraph definition of contribution; a budget page; Curator
terms that say what happens when the grant ends.

**A corridor speaks for its points, never for a place.** The
anthropologist's fourth scenario puts the project's own §3.2
question back to it — who said so, and who was not asked — and
finds the honest answer is twenty-two households inside a line of
1,900 residents. The rule that follows: no map derived from hosts'
consents is ever presented as a community position; it carries the
count of consents against residents inside the line, a separate
revocable consent for that use, and the hromada council's recorded
response. The white paper's §12 line was the breach, and is
corrected.

**Hosts are told a promise the map cannot keep.** "Withdraw a
place and it leaves every map and every export" is true of Plai
and only of Plai; once copied, a point is copied. The manual tells
entrants "the point is published where you put it, so take care"
and tells curators to check "Is the point where the place is?",
which pushes a displaced point back — so the meeting-place option
in the agreement is a sentence the manual contradicts. *Changes:*
the agreement and the sign-on sheet say plainly that withdrawal
removes a point from Plai, not from copies; the manual stops
contradicting the meeting-place option; "Name of the place"
guidance says no surname where the village is small.

And one thing all three say without being asked: the documents are
unusually honest about their own failure modes, and that honesty
is not mitigation. Each tester could break the design by quoting
it.

## 2. Stress test one — economic viability over five years

*Run as a sceptical programme officer at a foundation funding
rural and heritage work in Central and Eastern Europe.*

The paper itself gives no budget figure, no lodging price, no
traveler estimate (white paper §10: "What does not exist … Any
budget figure"; proposal, Funding: "Budget figures for each area,
and the pilot's length" are open), so every number attached to
money below is the tester's own illustrative assumption and is
labelled as such.

### Scenario A — "Nobody comes"

**What happens.** Year 1. A modest grant — call it €30,000, the
tester's figure — reaches the project. There is no legal body to
receive it (white paper §10: "'UBEC' initiates every document but
neither tree names its legal form, and the controller of every
host's data is today the project lead personally"; agreement A-1
open), so it lands with the lead personally or with a borrowed
fiscal host. The lead spends the autumn finding five hosts in the
Verkhovyna area, sitting beside them while they enter (Gate 3).
One Curator, a local teacher, is paid — the proposal names "A paid
role for the first Curator during the pilot" — say €400 a month,
the tester's figure, under no written terms yet (white paper §10
names "paid roles with no terms yet" as a risk; agreement §8 says
"Paid roles are written down" but the arrangement does not exist).
Walking the corridor, two legal reads, translation and hosting
take the rest.

Then the season. Western advisories stand (white paper §9.1).
Forty enquiries arrive in the year. By general experience of such
networks perhaps half become journeys: twenty parties, averaging
three nights each, sixty host-nights spread over five hosts —
twelve nights per host per year. At any plausible Hutsul village
contribution (the paper gives none; the tester takes 600 UAH a
night with a meal) a host earns around 7,000 UAH a year, roughly
€150 — less than the firewood for the guest room. The Curator, by
contrast, has drawn €4,800 from the grant to handle forty
conversations. The grant has paid the gatekeeper thirty times what
any gate earned.

Year 2: the grant ends. The Curator is unpaid; the agreement says
only "where funding allows, a paid one" (Curator part). The
Curator stops answering within a season — nothing in the design
pays them to continue. Hosts who were told "no calendar, no
ratings, no commission" now also have no travelers. Two withdraw
their places; nobody notices, because withdrawn places "leave
every map and every export" (§5) and no count of withdrawals is
published. By year 3 the map shows three points and a published
PDF.

**What breaks.** The project is the Goodwin & Santilli case it
cites (white paper §4.2). Pathways does not build beds — true —
but it builds a *paid role* before confirming travelers, and the
paid role is the only thing in the design that moves. The
project's own ADEPT lesson is unanswered: "the durable results
came from a payment stream tied to land practice, not from tourism
marketing or grant programmes alone" (§6). Pathways has no payment
stream of its own by design and no membership base of the Via
Transilvanica kind. After the grant, its running cost is carried
by nobody.

**The claim tested.** "The project will not promise a host an
income it cannot show" (white paper §12) is kept to the letter:
agreement §10 says "Nobody is promised a number of travelers, an
income, or that every host will be free." But the proposal says
hosts "gain a fair, direct connection to travelers" and
"Communities gain income that stays local". A host reads the
second sentence, not the tenth clause of the agreement. The honest
version — "we cannot show you a single traveler and may never" —
appears nowhere a host would read it at the first visit.

**Already answered.** Donor dependence named as a warning sign
(white paper §4.2); success "not counted in travelers" (§11); Gate
4 records "what was missing" (prompt §8); funders get "An open
account at each gate"; the Curator "where funding allows, a paid
one"; the proposal's funding table puts people before software.

**Missing.** Any exit for the Curator's pay; any definition of
what a host is told about expected demand; any threshold at which
the project says "stop"; any cost-carrier after the grant (the
paper's own §6 table marks this "Open"); a count of enquiries,
journeys, host-nights and withdrawals, published per gate.

**Required before funding.**
1. A one-page budget with the pilot's length, in proposal v0.3,
   before any funding note.
2. A written Curator arrangement (agreement §8) stating that pay
   ends with the grant and what the Curator does in year 2 unpaid,
   signed before Gate 3.
3. A "what we can show you" sheet read to every host at the first
   visit, in Ukrainian, with the enquiry and journey count to date
   (zero at Gate 3), appended to the sign-on sheet (agreement Part
   III).
4. Gate 4's record extended to money: enquiries, journeys,
   host-nights, host-reported earnings (aggregated, anonymous),
   Curator hours and pay (prompt §8).
5. A stop rule in prompt §8: if fewer than N journeys in two
   seasons, the paid role ends and the hosts are told the pilot is
   closed, their data handed back (§11).
6. A named cost-carrier after the grant — membership, partner
   initiative, or the lead's own commitment in writing — or the
   explicit statement that there is none.

### Scenario B — "Everybody comes"

**What happens.** The war freezes in year 2; accession advances; a
Guardian piece modelled on its Via Transilvanica coverage. Three
thousand travelers in one season on a corridor of twelve hosts.
Twelve hosts with capacity in "small whole numbers" (prompt §5) —
say four beds each, forty-eight beds a night. A four-to-six-day
walk (prompt §3) means each traveler wants four nights: twelve
thousand host-nights in a hundred-and-fifty-night season, eighty a
night against forty-eight beds. The corridor is overbooked before
the first host knows it.

The Curator must listen to each traveler, suggest only hosts with
room, tell those hosts in advance, brief each traveler, and keep
and delete each traveler's details (Curator part). Three thousand
parties in a hundred and fifty days is twenty conversations a day,
each a safety briefing in wartime. One person cannot do it. Three
things follow: the Curator stops answering; travelers go anyway,
because every published place shows "its point, its description
and its hosting details" to "Everyone" (§5); and the Curator opens
a spreadsheet of who is where on which night — a calendar by
another name, forbidden by principle and so kept private and
uncounted.

"No ratings" does not stop rating; it moves it. Travelers choose
by the Instagram geotag at the published point and by a Google
listing the host never asked for. The hosts nearest Verkhovyna
saturate; those a day off the line see no one — the paper's own
failure sign (§11). Stewards, unpaid and under no terms, are asked
to triage.

The land market. A map of houses "at their exact points" (OI-P7)
is, in the paper's own words, "also a scouting list" (§11).
"Within settlements, foreigners may already buy houses" (§1.4). In
year 4 two ageing hosts sell to people who first saw the house on
Plai. Their places are withdrawn and leave every export — so the
project cannot even show it happened.

**Does anything stop it?** No. The only hard instruments are the
Curator holding a place (§9) and the administrator closing an
account "for serious harm". Holding a place means switching off
the map once it works. "A limit for a stretch is open" (§11). The
meeting-place option exists, but "whether the Curator says this to
every host in those words is open". The Bergsteigerdörfer lesson
the paper draws — "a label is credible only if it can expel" (§6)
— has no corridor-level counterpart. Everything in Scenario B is
*described* in §11 and nothing is *prevented*.

**Missing.** A corridor ceiling; a second Curator and the rule for
appointing one; a published-map response to overload (the Curator
unpublishing hosting details and leaving only points); a default
of meeting-place placement for homes, which OI-P7 declined; any
monitoring of house sales along the corridor; any Phase 2
governance beyond "the hosts … are asked" (§4).

**Required before funding.**
1. A stretch ceiling (travelers per season or host-nights per
   host) in prompt §5 beside capacity, with the Curator's duty to
   decline above it.
2. The Curator's duties re-scoped for load: the briefing in
   writing (OI-P12 closed), a second Curator named at a stated
   enquiry count.
3. OI-P7 revisited: meeting-place placement as the default a
   Curator proposes to every home host, in those words, on the
   sign-on sheet.
4. A written response to off-platform ratings and geotags in the
   traveler part.
5. A land-watch: the Curator records, per gate, houses on the
   corridor sold and whether they were on the map (prompt §8).
6. Steward terms: §8 "Paid roles are written down" extended to
   unpaid ones — hours, insurance, what they are not.

### Scenario C — "The money has to move"

**One traveler, ten days.** A German walker, card only, no
hryvnia, enters by land. Night 1, Vorokhta: host chose "a fixed
contribution", 800 UAH, cash; she has none and pays after a town
ATM. Under Procedure 297 the host may lodge without registering as
a business if he declares the income annually (§9.3); whether he
declares, the project "cannot check … and must not seem to".
Nights 2–3, a polonyna family, "suggested contribution": she
leaves €40 in euro notes — foreign cash to a private person, in
practice undeclared; "A payment for a bed or a meal is income from
services whatever it is called" (§9.4); the meal is a
food-facility grey zone (§9.3). Nights 4–6, "exchange of work":
she helps with the hay — "in-kind income is taxable at fair value"
and a foreigner's work for board "raises questions of work permits
and volunteer law that remain unsettled" (§9.4); the New Zealand
precedent (§6). Nights 7–8, a host who chose "ubec-pay or a
community currency": nothing exists to pay with — "community
currencies have no legal status" (§9.4); she pays cash; the form
on the map was fiction. Nights 9–10, Kosiv workshop, cash. Tourist
tax: a grey zone for a non-registered host (§9.3) — collected
nowhere on the journey.

Meanwhile the Curator who suggested all five hosts holds "amounts"
(§5), was paid from the grant under no written terms, and is the
only person who knows which of the five declares. The funder asks
for receipts. The project holds no traveler money, so the funder's
receipts cover grant spend only — defensible — but the funder's
paid officer is the holder of knowledge about undeclared income
along the whole corridor. That is where the project is exposed:
not in a transaction it took, but in a person it pays.

**Contribution versus price.** In law, there is no difference
(§9.4). In practice, "a fixed contribution" is a price with a
kinder noun — the Camino drift the paper cites: "some private
owners call a fixed €12 'donativo'" (§6). Until "what makes a
contribution different from a price" (§11) is answered, the word
is marketing.

**A-3.** If payment passes "through the Curator", the Curator
assembles "two or more services" and takes money for them, which
on the paper's own reading of the Law on Tourism makes a *tourist
product* and a licensing question (§9.4). A-3's second option is
incompatible with agreement §10 ("not a tour operator"). Only
direct payment keeps the project what it says it is.

**Required before funding.**
1. A-3 closed as "directly from traveler to host", the "through
   the Curator" option struck from the agreement and the proposal
   (OI-P13).
2. "ubec-pay or a community currency" removed from the four forms
   until a lawful instrument exists, or defined in one paragraph
   of §8 (OI-P8).
3. A one-paragraph definition of "contribution" in agreement §8,
   readable by a host and a traveler; "fixed contribution" renamed
   or justified.
4. The Curator's written arrangement stating payer, status under
   Ukrainian tax, hours, and that the Curator records no host
   amounts beyond what a journey needs.
5. A legal body or fiscal sponsor named (A-1) as grantee, before
   any money moves.
6. The Ukrainian tourism-law read completed and summarised in
   white paper §9.4, with "exchange of work" for foreigners either
   kept in the lawyer's words or dropped for non-residents.
7. A cash-and-currency note in the traveler briefing (OI-P12).

### Verdict

**Fund conditionally — a small planning-and-legal grant, not the
pilot.** The thinking is unusually honest: the paper names its own
failure modes with sources, refuses to count in travelers, and
keeps its open items open. But honesty about risk is not
mitigation of it. Scenario A shows the only moving part — a paid
Curator — is funded before a single traveler exists, with no exit
and no cost-carrier after the grant. Scenario B shows every
overload and land-market harm is described in §11 and prevented by
nothing. Scenario C shows the money design is three unlawful or
undefined forms out of four, a payer that does not legally exist,
and a paid officer holding tax knowledge. Fund A-1, the two legal
reads, the budget page, the Curator terms, the closing of A-3,
OI-P8 and OI-P12 and a stretch ceiling against a six-month
deadline, and only then consider the pilot, with Curator pay
capped to the grant period and a published stop rule. Refuse any
request to fund hosts' income, software, or a second corridor
until Gate 4 has been walked and its money record published.

## 3. Stress test two — adversaries and failures

*Run as a red-team reviewer with experience of security and
data-protection failures in civic-tech projects in conflict zones.
Software capabilities cited are only those the manual, the
specification and the mapservice state review state.*

### Scenario A — "The scraper"

**What happens.** A land-listing aggregator in Lviv, or a hostile
OSINT cell, opens the public map without logging in. The public
Plai copy is a MapServer WMS behind Mapbender: a GetMap image and
a GetFeatureInfo click. Hosts are drawn in a colour of their own —
"A host, a place with at least one offer, is drawn in ochre, the
other places in red" (manual §6) — so a script needs no click to
find them: it pulls tiles over the corridor, converts every ochre
pixel to a coordinate, then clicks each one. The click returns
"its name, type, category, offering, offers, months, languages,
how to reach it, capacities and forms of payment" (manual §6).
None of that is consent-gated, by decision (spec §5, Q3: offers,
season, capacity, payment forms, languages and reach "describe the
place and its terms, not the person"). With five hosts it is an
afternoon by hand; with fifty, a shell loop. No document mentions
a rate limit, a request cap or an alert on GetFeatureInfo volume,
and the agreement notes "short logs are a principle of the service
not yet put in place" (§5).

**What the record yields.** For each ochre point: exact
coordinates of a dwelling; a name that is very often a person's —
the manual's own example is "Ivan's farm" (§4), which makes the
"My contact name may be shown" gate decorative; lodging plus
"Languages you speak: en, de, pl"; months open (when strangers
with cash are in the house, and when it may be empty); beds; "By
car" (a road reaches it); a photo if agreed. A land-listing site
gets a scouting list of old houses with owners evidently open to
outsiders and short of income — the white paper's own failure sign
(§11). A hostile state actor gets a list of private homes in a
controlled border district that receive foreigners, with occupancy
by month. The realistic hostile uses are intelligence,
denunciation and later pressure rather than a strike; the
project's own words still apply: "for a home, in wartime, with a
name beside it, that point is an address" (§7).

**Withdrawal once copied.** "Withdraw a place and it leaves every
map and every export" (§5) is true of Plai and only of Plai; the
manual is honestly narrow — "Nothing is deleted; you can send it
again later" (§5). Refusing an open licence (A-2) controls lawful
reuse, not copying; the scraper never needed a licence. The
agreement binds only its own contributors not to put hosts in
OpenStreetMap.

**The meeting-place mitigation, tested.** "A host may instead
place their point at a nearby meeting place — a crossroads, a
chapel, a shop — and tell travelers the rest in person" (§7). It
fails on four counts. (1) The manual instructs the opposite: the
entrant is told "The point is published where you put it, so take
care" (§3) and the curator's checklist asks "Is the point where
the place is?" (§7); a curator following the manual pushes a
displaced point back. (2) In a hamlet of scattered farmsteads, a
chapel 300 m away plus "Ivan's farm" plus a photo of the house is
no displacement. (3) The ochre colour still says "a home in this
village receives foreigners". (4) Whether anyone even tells hosts
is open (white paper §11). The mitigation is a sentence, not a
procedure.

**Already answered.** Exact point as informed choice (OI-P7;
review R-7); the sign-on acknowledgement; no open licence (A-2);
the Pathways map as "a backend copy of Plai with its own audience
… whose security tab grants view to the public, logged-in users,
groups or single users" (prompt §2) — a lever that exists and is
unused.

**Must change.**
1. *Agreement §5 and Part III:* "Once published, a point may have
   been copied by others; withdrawal removes it from Plai, not
   from copies."
2. *Decision:* for the pilot, grant view of Plai's entry layer to
   logged-in users only, with the base map public. Travelers reach
   hosts through the Curator anyway, by design. If host points
   must be public, revisit spec Q4: a style on one layer cannot be
   unchecked in a copy; a second layer could.
3. *Manual §3 and §7:* replace "Is the point where the place is?"
   with "Is the point where the host chose to be found?", and put
   the meeting-place option in the entrant chapter and the
   curator's first-visit script.
4. *Manual §4:* "Name of the place" guidance — no surname, no
   first name where the village is small.
5. *Plai/ops:* enable the abuse-control logging the agreement
   already permits, and a request threshold that pages the
   administrator.

### Scenario B — "The lead is unreachable"

**What happens.** The lead is detained at a checkpoint,
hospitalised, or offline in a blackout, for six weeks. Day 3: a
host in Dzembronia receives a threatening call referencing "the
foreigners you keep" and writes to the stewardship address asking
for her place to be gone. Day 10: a traveler is on stage two. Day
30: a funder wants the Gate 3 account. The review foresaw it
(R-1).

**What is actually possible.** If the host holds her own account
and password, she can withdraw herself (manual §5): the "at once"
promise holds. If her place was entered for her, only the Curator
can withdraw their own row or set it back to Submitted (§7). So
the at-once promise survives the lead's absence *only if the
Curator is a different, reachable person*. No Curator yet exists
by name (white paper §10); if the lead is also the first Curator,
everything stops. If she has forgotten her password: "the service
sends no email yet. Write to stewardship@ubec.network instead"
(manual §1) — an inbox nobody reads.

**What is broken.** "Delete places and accounts within 30 days of
a request" — only the administrator purges. "If your data is
exposed … you are told promptly" — nobody to tell. The GDPR's
72-hour breach notice — nobody. §12 disputes escalate "to the
project lead" — dead end. §4: "the project lead decides" —
governance halts. A disk failure: backups are a stated condition,
not a fact, and even when made, "`backup.py` cannot dump
Mapbender's own database, where every account lives" (state review
§2) — a restore brings back places and loses every login. Who pays
the hosting and domain invoices in week six is written nowhere.
A-1 means the data controller is a natural person who is
unreachable.

**Must change before Gate 3.**
1. *Decision:* name the deputy administrator (A-4) with a
   Mapbender backend account, a sealed copy of the backup key, and
   the hoster and registrar credentials; record it in the prompt.
2. *Decision:* the first Curator is not the lead.
3. *Ops:* the stewardship address becomes a shared or forwarded
   mailbox read by the deputy; the manual's "Help" line names
   both.
4. *Plai:* extend the backup to Mapbender's own database (state
   review F5) before the first real account.
5. *Agreement §11:* "If the lead is unreachable for 14 days, the
   deputy acts as administrator and the first Curator decides what
   the lead would have decided, on the record."

### Scenario C — "The bad guest, the bad host"

**What happens (guest).** A traveler stays two nights, leaves with
a lizhnyk and posts the house on Instagram, geotagged, "staying
with Maria in Kryvorivnia". The agreement asked him to "never
publish where a host lives" — a promise with no account, no
identity check, no sanction. The Curator held his name and contact
and must delete them "once their journey is over" (A-6 open). If
deletion was prompt, the only record of who he was is gone before
the host finds the post.

**What happens (host).** A traveler reports that a host made
unwanted advances. §9: told to the Curator, heard in confidence;
the Curator may hold a host's place or decline to suggest; the
administrator may close an account for serious harm.

**Who learns what.** The Curator, and nobody else. "No ratings"
means the next traveler is warned only by the Curator's silent
non-suggestion. But the host's point stays public unless the
Curator sets it to Submitted — a visible act the host will contest
— and the public map lets travelers bypass the Curator entirely. A
traveler who finds the ochre point and knocks has had no briefing,
no screening, no record; the host's promised protection — "The
Curator speaking with every traveler before suggesting you, and
telling you who is coming" — does not reach them. The design
assumes mediation; the map does not enforce it.

**How the next host is warned.** By the Curator's memory. Keeping
a note of a banned traveler is a data set with no basis in §5
("For nothing else") and no retention rule; passing it to "the
next Curator" is undefined. "The Curator as memory" is here
literal and single-point.

**Evidence.** For the theft: whatever the Curator had not yet
deleted, and the host's guest register under Procedure 297 if she
keeps one. §7 "never shared beyond it" sits awkwardly against
telling the police; §5's "unless the law compels it" is about
authorities demanding data, not a victim wanting it given.

**Where refusals meet duty of care.** No ratings is defensible; no
*record* is not. "Each of us answers for our own actions" (§10)
does not protect a Curator who suggests, next season, a host they
were told harmed someone. The white paper cites Servas, where
"applicants interviewed by a national committee" (§6), and adopts
nothing from it.

**Must change before the first traveler.**
1. *Agreement §9:* an incident record — what, when, who reported,
   what was done — kept by the Curator and the deputy, minimal,
   with a stated retention under A-6, named in §5 as a purpose.
   Not public, not a rating.
2. *Traveler part:* identity is seen (a passport name and photo,
   not stored beyond A-6), a short conversation before suggestion,
   and the host may ask to see the same document on arrival. The
   host's part gains: "You may ask a traveler who did not come
   through the Curator to contact the Curator first."
3. *Agreement §7:* "Confidential means confidential" gains "except
   to protect someone from harm, or to report a crime to the
   authorities with the harmed person's agreement."
4. *Decision — the bypass:* either host points are not public in
   the pilot, or the public click carries one line, "Travelers:
   contact the Curator at … before visiting" — a click-label
   change with no new mechanism (spec §5).
5. *Project doc:* a one-page response plan for a published home —
   who contacts the platform for takedown, who tells the host that
   withdrawal from Plai does nothing to the post.

### Scenario D — "The border and the permit"

**What happens.** A German traveler, briefed by message, walks
from a Dzembronia host toward Pip Ivan Chornohirskyi. The stage
lies inside the regime (white paper §9.2). A patrol detains him.
He has no permit because how a foreigner obtains one "no source
answered". The host who pointed him up the valley and the Curator
whose messages suggested the stage are questioned; the Curator's
phone holds a list of homes receiving foreigners.

**The two promises, against reality.** "A stage that needs a
permit says so before anyone walks it" (§7): says so *where*?
"Only points. Plai records each place as a single point. It offers
no lines or areas" (manual §3). Stages exist nowhere but the
Curator's head and messages. "Safety knowledge stays with the
Curator … border-zone rules … never published" (§7) confuses two
things. The permit regime is public law; keeping it unpublished
protects no one and guarantees that a traveler who bypasses the
Curator walks in ignorant. What should be confidential is the
*current* state — closures, incidents, patrol practice — not the
rule. Worse, a published "Viewpoint" on Pip Ivan is an invitation
into a restricted zone with no field in the click to say so.

**Who answers.** "A Curator's route is a suggestion made in good
faith … walking it is each traveler's own choice" (§10) is
civil-liability language. The detachment's practice, by the
paper's own source, is that "group leaders send route, dates and
the passport data of participants to the detachment in advance"
(§9.2): in the detachment's eyes the Curator *is* the organiser.
The Curator's protection is a written stage sheet and a written
briefing — neither exists.

**Must change before the first traveler.**
1. *Decision:* no stage inside the five-kilometre strip is
   suggested to a foreign traveler until the Curator holds, in
   writing from the detachment, how a foreigner obtains the permit
   and has done it once.
2. *Curator's kit:* a stage register, one sheet per stage — start,
   end, walking time, inside or outside the strip (confirmed with
   the detachment by village), permit needed and how, current
   closures, date last checked. This is what "says so" means. Gate
   4 fills it.
3. *Agreement §7:* split the sentence — the permit regime is
   public and told to every traveler in writing; the current state
   and incidents are the Curator's and confidential.
4. *Briefing in writing:* the traveler acknowledges by message the
   stage sheet's permit line before walking; the Curator keeps
   that acknowledgement for the duration of A-6.
5. *Plai rule:* no place along the way inside the strip is
   published; the Curator holds it (Submitted), and the click
   gains a "Border zone — permit needed" label only if a later
   migration adds the field.

### The five changes that matter most, ranked

1. **Decide the bypass.** Host points visible to logged-in viewers
   only during the pilot, or a Curator-contact line in every
   click. Every safety promise assumes the Curator stands between
   map and door; today nothing makes it so. (A, C, D)
2. **Name the deputy and separate the roles.** Deputy
   administrator with key and credentials (A-4), a shared
   stewardship mailbox, a first Curator who is not the lead,
   Mapbender's database in the backup. (B)
3. **Tell hosts the truth about copies.** Withdrawal removes from
   Plai, not from the world; names in "Name of the place" are a
   choice; the meeting-place option is offered to every host and
   the manual stops contradicting it. (A)
4. **Keep a minimal incident record and see a passport.** Not
   ratings: a confidential log with retention under A-6, a named
   exception to confidentiality for harm and crime, an identity
   seen before a journey is suggested. (C)
5. **Build the stage register before anyone walks.** Written stage
   sheets with the strip boundary confirmed village by village,
   the permit regime told in writing to every traveler, no stage
   in the strip for foreigners until the detachment has said how.
   (D)

## 4. Stress test three — capture and co-option

*Run as an anthropologist of rural development who has watched
community projects in mountain villages be captured, co-opted or
hollowed out. A preliminary from the tester: the documents are
unusually honest; they name the Curator's power, the founder's
position and the corridor problem as open tensions. But naming a
tension is not a safeguard, and in each scenario the project's own
honesty becomes the weapon — a capturer need only quote the
documents.*

### Scenario A — "The Curator's village"

**What happens.** Gate 3 names one Curator: a respected teacher in
a village between Verkhovyna and Kosiv, chosen because the lead
knows and trusts her. She is good. She finds the first five hosts
in a month, because she knows who has a spare room: a
sister-in-law, a cousin who makes lizhnyky, two neighbours. By
year two there are fourteen hosts; eleven live within four
kilometres of her house. A host from the next village, from a
family with an old quarrel over a polonyna boundary, enters a
place; it stays "sent to the Curator" for nine months. Asked, the
Curator says the point looked wrong and the stage is "not safe
this season" — and under §7 she owes no reason. Her cousin opens a
shop at the one crossroads every suggested route passes. No fee
changes hands. Travelers love it. The lead sees a living network.
The next village sees what it has always seen: the family that got
the project.

**What breaks.** "No one speaks for a community" (white paper
§5.3) is true and useless here. Nobody spoke for anyone; a kinship
network became the map. This is the oldest finding in the
literature the paper cites: Mansuri and Rao's induced
participation "often dominated by elites" unless there is "a
responsive centre and a long commitment" — and the responsive
centre here is one foreigner who chose the Curator. Platteau's
point is sharper: elite capture is rarely theft; it is a respected
broker allocating a scarce good — here travelers and visibility —
along existing lines of obligation. From general knowledge:
Nepal's community forest groups, Kyrgyz community-tourism
associations and the Maramureș guesthouse networks show the same
curve — the first intermediary's household and village absorb the
benefit within two or three years, and the rules see nothing
because nothing was formally broken.

**Already answered.** The Curator "takes nothing from a host for
sending travelers" (§8) — a cousin's shop is none of "gift, fee or
favour". Graduated sanctions (white paper §7.3) are the Curator's
sanctions on hosts; nothing sanctions a Curator short of closing
an account "for serious harm", which a nine-month hold is not. The
dispute route (§12) sends the passed-over host first to the person
who held him. "Final word on what is published … within this
agreement" has no content, because the agreement nowhere says when
a hold must be lifted or explained. The white paper admits it:
"How a Curator is chosen and by whom, and what a host passed over
can do, are open" (§11).

**Missing.** A way to choose a Curator that is not the lead's
trust. A time limit and a reason on a hold. A route around the
Curator. Any sanction on a Curator between "nothing" and "closed
account". Any rule on Curators' and stewards' close relatives as
hosts. And a fact the prompt §4 records: for a host whose place
was entered for them, "the host cannot edit or withdraw it
themselves" — the people most likely to be entered this way are
the old and unconnected, the people least able to resist.

**Should change.**
1. *Agreement §7 and Curator part:* a hold on a *host's place*
   beyond 30 days carries a written reason to the host and the
   lead; beyond 90 days it is reviewed by someone other than the
   Curator. *Trade-off:* Curators must write down judgments about
   neighbours they would rather keep oral; some will refuse the
   role.
2. *Agreement §12:* a passed-over host may go directly to the lead
   (Phase 1) or to a second Curator or steward (Phase 2), skipping
   the Curator of their stretch. *Trade-off:* it undercuts the
   Curator in exactly the cases where a bad host is rightly
   refused.
3. *New rule:* a Curator or steward declares kin and household
   relations to hosts on their stretch; a stretch where more than
   a third of hosts are the Curator's close kin is a flag the lead
   must answer in writing at the next gate. *Trade-off:* in Hutsul
   villages everyone is kin to someone; a third is arbitrary and
   will be resented as an outsider's suspicion.
4. *Gate 3 decision:* name the first Curator only after the three
   to five hosts, not before, and have the hosts say who they
   would trust — a Curator proposed by hosts rather than for them.
   *Trade-off:* slower, and five hosts are not a village either.
5. *Gate 4:* the walk records the distribution of hosts by
   village, not only distance and time.

### Scenario B — "Pathways™"

**What happens.** Year two. The oblast tourism department asks to
put the "living network" on the oblast map with its logo beside
Bukovel's. A hromada head asks for the same. A developer's
brochure for twelve chalets above Mykulychyn appears with the
headings "Honest offer", "A pathway of relationships" and
"Corridor of stewardship", attributed, correctly, to a CC BY-SA
document. A European foundation offers €400,000 to "scale the
model across the arc" — with an app and a modest rating. The lead
says no. The foundation finds a Romanian partner, forks the
documents (lawfully), launches "Carpathian Pathways" with a
calendar, and is cited at the next C8 summit as the
community-tourism success story.

**What breaks.** The project has deliberately kept everything that
would let it refuse — a legal body, a trademark, a closed licence
on its words — out of its design, for good reasons. Its only
defence against co-option is a sentence.

**Already answered.** §6: "nobody may use its name to suggest that
the project endorses something it has not" — binds signatories
only. Documents and emblem are CC BY-SA: ShareAlike compels a
forker to stay open, not to keep "no calendar". The partner clause
asks a partner to keep "Part I's commitments on data, consent,
safety and money" — no calendar, no ratings, no commission are in
§3, not in that list; a partner may lawfully keep the data rules
and add a booking grid. Without a legal body (white paper §10)
there is no party to a trademark, a licence dispute or a
partnership note. The Bergsteigerdörfer lesson — "a label is
credible only if it can expel" — has no label to expel from.

**Missing.** A name the project can defend; what a partner must
*not* do; a public register of who is and is not a partner; a
settled answer on whether the project wants to be on anyone's
tourism map at all. From general knowledge: this is how
"ecomuseum", "slow", "agrotourism" and "community-based" were
hollowed in a decade each — not by theft but by free adoption of
the vocabulary by actors with marketing budgets.

**Should change.**
1. *Decision (A-1):* form the legal body before Gate 3, however
   small, and register the name and the Plai emblem as marks in
   Ukraine and the EU; keep the *documents* CC BY-SA but move the
   name and mark to a trademark policy that permits use only by
   signed co-creators and partners. *Trade-off:* a legal body is
   cost, bureaucracy and a board; it is the first step towards the
   institution the project said it did not want to be.
2. *Partner clause:* "keeps Part I's commitments on data, consent,
   safety and money" becomes "keeps Part I in full, §3 included" —
   a partner with a calendar or ratings is not a partner.
   *Trade-off:* fewer partners, and some good initiatives already
   run a booking page.
3. *New public page:* a list of signed partners and a dated list
   of requests declined, in both languages. Refusal by publication
   is the only refusal available to a body without lawyers.
   *Trade-off:* it makes enemies in the oblast administration the
   hosts must live with.
4. *White paper §12:* decide whether "a place in the Carpathian
   Eight's … work" means appearing on institutional maps. Say so
   either way. *Trade-off:* refusing visibility refuses travelers
   and funders; accepting it hands the frame to the mapmaker.

### Scenario C — "The founder"

**What happens.** Year three. Twenty hosts, two Curators, a walked
corridor, a grant that ends in six months. The corridor's people
meet, as §4 promised, and say: we want a modest fee from travelers
to pay the Curator; we want a calendar because answering the same
question forty times is not "memory"; some want reviews, because a
traveler stole from a host and the next host had no way of
knowing. The lead hears a betrayal of §3 and says the principles
"decide every choice in the project". The hosts answer, in
Ukrainian, that they are the project. Variant two: the lead is
unreachable for a month and no deputy was named. Variant three:
nothing dramatic; it is 2029 and every gate still opens "on
Michel's word".

**What breaks.** "Organic growth … not from a plan drawn above it"
describes the *route*; it has never described the *rules*, which
were all written by one person above it, in English first. §4's
promise is precise and the hosts should read it precisely: they
"are asked how the project should be governed from there". Asked.
Blackstock's critique applies to the project's own founder: the
community treated as "a homogeneous bloc" that will either confirm
the principles or betray them, consulted on governance, not vested
with it. From general knowledge: founder's syndrome kills more
village projects than elite capture, and its usual form is not
tyranny but a founder who is right about the principles and wrong
about who owns them.

**Already answered.** §4: Phase 1 has no end date; a gate "opens
on Michel's word". §1: the project is "led by Michel Garand", who
answers for it and its data; the instance, server, backups and
marks are therefore the founder's personally. §11: the deputy
(A-4) open; if Plai must stop, three months' notice and a copy of
data — an individual exit, not a collective one: twenty hosts may
each take their row home; they cannot take the map. A partner may
leave with its corridor's data; the first corridor has no partner
note and so no such right. White paper §7.3 concedes: "Collective
choice is the one principle the pilot defers." §13: the hosts'
remedy against a founder is exit.

**Missing.** When Phase 1 ends. Any binding force in the Phase 2
promise. Who owns the instance and the name if corridor and
founder part. Which principles the corridor may change and which
it may not — "all" and "none" are both wrong. What "the corridor's
own Curators choosing the next corridor" requires: a body that can
sign a partnership note, hold a budget and make accounts, none of
which a Plai login confers.

**Should change.**
1. *Agreement §4:* Phase 1 ends at a stated event — Gate 4 walked,
   or the tenth host, or 24 months after Gate 3, whichever first —
   not on the lead's word. *Trade-off:* the lead loses the power
   to delay a hand-over to people who are not ready.
2. *Agreement §4:* replace "are asked" with a decision rule: the
   corridor's hosts, stewards and Curators *decide* Phase 2
   governance by a method they choose, with two entrenched clauses
   only — §5 (data stays with the people) and §9 (how we treat
   each other). Everything else in §3, calendar and ratings
   included, becomes theirs to keep or change. *Trade-off:* the
   project may become, lawfully and from the inside, the booking
   service it was built to refuse. That is the price of meaning
   "organic growth".
3. *Decision (A-1, A-4):* the legal body, not the person, becomes
   controller and holder of the Plai instance and the marks, with
   a named deputy and a second key-holder from the corridor,
   before any real account. *Trade-off:* it ends the founder's
   unilateral ability to switch the thing off.
4. *Partner part extended to the first corridor:* a partnership
   note with the first corridor itself, so it has the same right
   to leave with its data, map and name as a Romanian partner
   would. *Trade-off:* the founder signs away the possibility of
   the corridor forking against him.

### Scenario D — "The corridor on the minister's desk"

**What happens.** Year three. Twenty-two hosts have ticked a new
consent for "stewardship corridor" use; three declined and stay on
the map. Their points, with the paths between, trace a band from
the Chornohora foothills to the Cheremosh. A friendly official in
the Convention secretariat asks for it. The band is placed in a
submission on cultural landscapes for COP8. Within a year: a wind
developer's lawyer notes that of roughly 1,900 residents inside
the line, 22 consented, and that the "corridor" is twenty-two
households' tourism preferences dressed as landscape policy. A
woman whose meadow lies inside the band finds a buyer's agent at
her gate and a forester refusing a mowing permit, both citing "the
corridor". The Verkhovyna hromada council, never asked, resolves
that nobody speaks for the hromada's land but the hromada.

**What breaks.** The question the project wrote §3.2 to ask of
others — who said so, and who was not asked — is asked of the
project, and the answer is: twenty-two people, chosen by a Curator
(Scenario A), none a landowner inside the line who was not also a
host. Rambaldi's ethics of participatory mapping, which the paper
cites, are precisely about this. A polygon on a minister's desk is
maximal precision. The council's objection is not elite capture;
it is an elected body saying the project did what it said no one
should do.

**Already answered.** White paper §8: "A corridor is not drawn by
the project. It emerges from the places people chose to share and
speaks only for them." The second sentence is honest; the first
not quite — someone draws the line between the points. §8's three
conditions; agreement §5 "For nothing else" and §13 consent again;
reasoning T-10 and T-12 (open); white paper §5.4 "hands data to no
one, an authority included, unless the law compels it" — a
submission is a voluntary hand-over the sentence does not
contemplate.

**Missing.** The honest answer: a map of individual consents
cannot be a community position, and the documents already know it
— "speaks only for them" means *not for the community*. What is
missing is the rule that follows: the project may never present a
corridor as what a community wants; it may present it as what
twenty-two named households do on their land. Also missing: the
hromada council, which the documents never mention although it is
the one body with a legal claim to speak for the land; the owners
inside the line who are not hosts; and the asymmetry between
consent to be *shown* and consent to be *used as evidence*, which
the agreement folds into one tick.

**Should change.**
1. *White paper §8 and §12:* a corridor is submitted, if at all,
   as "the places of these hosts and the paths between them", with
   the number of consents and the number of residents inside the
   line stated on the same page. *(Done in white paper v0.2 for
   §12.)* *Trade-off:* it makes the corridor worthless as
   advocacy, which is exactly what it should be worth.
2. *New agreement clause (T-10):* a separate, revocable consent
   for "use in a submission to a public body", distinct from the
   map consent, listing the body and the date; withdrawal removes
   the place from the submission's next version. *Trade-off:* a
   submission with holes in it.
3. *Governance rule:* no corridor leaves the project without the
   hromada council of each hromada it crosses having been shown it
   and given a written chance to object, and the objection travels
   with the map. *Trade-off:* a veto-by-delay to exactly the local
   officials §3.2 distrusts. Still better than being them.
4. *Decision:* publish corridors, if at all, as fuzzy bands or
   named stretches, never as parcels or polygons that a planner,
   lawyer or buyer can lay over the cadastre. *Trade-off:*
   fuzziness weakens the one use the paper hoped for.

### Three rules before Gate 3

1. **No Curator without a peer and a clock.** Every Curator is
   proposed by the hosts of the stretch, not by the lead; serves a
   fixed term renewable by those hosts; every hold on a host's
   place carries a written reason after 30 days and a review by
   someone other than the Curator after 90. *Trade-off:* the first
   Curator will be harder to find and slower to name, and the role
   loses the frank, oral authority that makes a good Curator good.
2. **The body before the hosts, and the hand-over written into
   it.** A legal body holds the instance, the marks and the
   controller role before the first real account; its founding
   document states that Phase 1 ends at a named event and that
   Phase 2 governance is decided, not "asked", by the first
   corridor's hosts, stewards and Curators, with only §5 and §9
   entrenched. *Trade-off:* the founder gives up the right to stop
   the corridor from becoming something he considers a betrayal,
   and takes on a board, accounts and a registry before a single
   host has signed.
3. **A corridor speaks for its points, never for a place.** No map
   derived from hosts' consents is presented to any public body or
   funder as a community position; every such map carries the
   count of consents against residents inside the line, a separate
   revocable consent for that use, and the hromada council's
   recorded response. *Trade-off:* the corridor becomes nearly
   useless as a weapon against a wind farm or a resort, which was
   half the reason to want it. The project keeps its one principle
   that cannot be captured — that it speaks for itself — at the
   cost of the influence it hoped that principle would earn.
