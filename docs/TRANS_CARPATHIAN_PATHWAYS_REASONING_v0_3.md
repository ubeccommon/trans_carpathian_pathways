*The proposal says what Trans-Carpathian Pathways is. The starting
prompt records what has been decided. The co-creator agreement
says what each of us promises. This document says why: the
pressures the project answers, the vision it serves, the reason
behind each principle, and the tensions it has to face honestly.
Written from a conversation with Michel on October 9, 2026.*

*Where a line is Michel's direction, it says so. Where it is
Claude's proposal, it says so. Lines marked neither restate what
the project's other documents already record. Nothing here is
decided by being written down: the open tensions in §7 wait on

## 1. Roots

*Claude's proposal, for Michel's word.*

**Plai.** In the speech of these mountains a *plai* is a mountain
path, the kind that follows a ridge from one pasture to the next.
The name of the map the project runs on is already its philosophy:
a path made by people walking it, between places where people live
and work.

**Ubuntu.** UBEC is the Ubuntu Bioregional Economic Commons.
*Ubuntu* is usually rendered "I am because we are": a person
becomes a person through other people. The project takes it one
step further, to the land: we are because the land is, and the
land as it is today is because people have lived with it.

**The bioregion.** The Carpathian arc is one living region across
the borders of seven countries or more. Its meadows, forests,
villages and crafts share one history and face one set of
pressures. The project is bioregional before it is national: it
starts in one corridor in Ukraine and is meant to cross every
border on the arc.

## 2. Why Now — Two Forces

*Michel's direction, October 9, 2026. The figures are sourced at
the end; what has no source is stated as Michel's observation in
the region.*

### The first force: the mountains re-valued from outside

The Carpathians are being re-valued from outside faster than from
within.

- **Tourism and property.** Resort property in Bukovel now costs
  more per square metre than new flats in Kyiv: quality complexes
  start at about $2,500 a square metre, some apart-hotels at
  $6,000, against a Kyiv median of about $1,400 in May 2026. The
  pressure is not confined to the resort: Verkhovyna is listed
  among the most expensive places to buy a house in the Ukrainian
  Carpathians, with prices up 10–15% on spring 2025.
- **Energy and industry on the high meadows.** Wind farms are
  planned or begun on polonynas that have never carried industry —
  Borzhava, Runa, Svydovets and other ridges — and a mega-resort
  is proposed on Svydovets, one of the best-preserved massifs of
  the Ukrainian Carpathians. Environmental groups report
  environmental assessment bypassed and oversight weakened under
  martial law.
- **The European Union.** Ukraine's path into the EU will open its
  mountain land to buyers who have never walked it. (Michel's
  observation; an expectation, not yet a measured effect.)
- **The arc as a political region.** In September 2026 Ukraine
  launched the Carpathian Eight, counting Romania, Serbia, Poland,
  Slovakia, Czechia, Austria and Hungary with the European Union,
  at a first summit in Bukovel; six states signed its
  declaration, and Hungary says it is not a member. Its stated agenda is
  security, energy, logistics and cross-border trade, and support
  for the region's communities; more than half of the fifty
  agreements signed there concerned solar and wind power. Cultural
  and natural heritage are not on its headline agenda.
- **Ageing holders.** The people who hold the old way of life —
  its houses, meadows, crafts and knowledge — are growing old, and
  their land will change hands within a generation. When it does,
  the knowledge goes with them unless someone is there to receive
  it. (Michel's observation.)
- **Not only Ukraine.** The same pressure has come before to the
  other countries of the arc. (Michel's observation.)

The pressure falls on cultural heritage and natural heritage
together, because in the Carpathians they are one thing: the
meadow exists because it was mown and grazed, the craft because it
had a use in a household that lived from the land.

### The second force: the people who keep the mountains alive

At the same time there is a second movement. Initiatives,
communities, young families and single people are stewards of the
cultural heritage and of the landscape. People are leaving the
cities to learn and live the Hutsul way of life — not to look at
it. Many of them are the people whose voice was not asked when the
mountains' future was planned. (Michel's direction.)

The two forces decide between them whether the Carpathians stay a
place where people live, or become a place people visit.

### Who speaks for a community

When a resort or a wind farm is said to have local support, the
first question is who said so, and who was not asked. *(Michel's
direction.)* The voice that reaches the newspapers is usually the
voice of those who stand to gain; it is the same story all over
the world. An example from the record: a report from the Yasinia
and Ust-Chorna communities says local leaders and residents still
hope for the Svydovets resort, for its taxes — without saying
which residents were asked — and in turn casts the resort's
opponents as serving logging interests. Each side
says the other's community voice is a cover for interests.

So the question is put in every direction: to a project's
supporters, to its opponents, and to this project. Across the
Carpathians, the people whose voice was passed over are finding
each other and finding initiatives that will listen, and the
tension between them and those who speak in their name is growing.
Pathways does not claim to speak for any village. It is built so
that no one has to.

### Where Pathways stands

Pathways stands with the second movement. It makes the value of a
way of life lived visible, and makes it pay, where it is lived —
an alternative that earns, not a lament. It is not against the
Carpathian Eight: the C8 promises support for the region's
communities, and Pathways is one concrete form that support can
take, built from the communities outward. *(Claude's proposal for
how the project names its relation to the C8.)*

## 3. The Diagnosis

*From the proposal's "The need"; the argument for why encounter
answers both is Claude's proposal.*

Two problems meet in the Carpathians, and the project's claim is
that they share one answer.

**Travel has become extraction.** Travelers move through places
without touching them; hosts perform hospitality instead of
sharing it; the meeting between a person and a place has become a
transaction. What a traveler takes away is a view, and what stays
behind is a fee, most of it to someone who does not live there.

**Mountain life is thinning out.** Villages lose people,
traditional knowledge goes unpassed, and the economy that
sustained a way of life has moved elsewhere.

**Why encounter answers both.** Knowledge of a craft, a meadow or
a path passes from person to person, by doing it beside someone;
it cannot be kept in a museum without becoming something else. A
pilgrim who sleeps under someone's roof, eats at their table and
learns how something is made leaves money and attention where they
are needed, in the hands of the person who holds the knowledge. A
host who shares what they actually have gains a reason to keep it,
and a means to. Encounter is the one form of travel that pays the
keeping of a way of life rather than its display.

**The condition.** It works only if the encounter is real. Once a
host performs, the knowledge being paid for is the performance,
and the way of life is still lost — more slowly and more
profitably. Every principle below guards that condition.

## 4. From Places to Corridors

*Michel's direction, October 9, 2026.*

Each host speaks for their own place. Placed side by side, those
places and the paths between them begin to say something none of
them says alone: where a living culture, a landscape and its
nature still hold together, and where they are most at risk. Over
time, the pathway will trace corridors — stretches of the arc
where cultural, natural and landscape heritage need stewardship as
one whole, and sensitive areas that need comprehensive
stewardship.

These are not wilderness areas. The Carpathian meadows, forests
and villages were not found untouched; they were made over
centuries by people living with the land, and they last only as
long as that relationship lasts. To protect nature by removing
people from it would end the very life that shaped it. The
stewardship these corridors call for is grounded in spirituality,
in connection, and in the relationships people have with each
other and with the land — in the understanding that people and
land are one and the same.

Such a corridor is not drawn by the project. It emerges from the
places people chose to share, and speaks only for them. Where it
gives a community the means to say what its land is worth before
others decide it for them, it has done its work.

**The pilgrimage.** This is what the journey is to. There is no
shrine at the end of the path. A pilgrim walks into a
relationship between people and land that has lasted centuries, is
received into it for a night or a month, and leaves having taken
part in it rather than having looked at it. The pilgrimage is to
the relationship itself. *(Claude's proposal for the wording.
"Pilgrimage" is kept, on Michel's word of October 10, 2026:
whoever comes is the Pilgrim of the Plai, in Ukrainian *прочанин
плаю*. The plai is the old path through the high pastures, and
the pilgrimage is to it and to the people who keep it. §7, T-3.)*

**A footing others recognise.** The vision has a vocabulary that
governments and funders already use. IUCN's Category V, the
*protected landscape*, is for areas whose character comes from
people and nature together over time — not wilderness. The
Carpathian Convention, adopted in Kyiv in 2003, binds seven of the
arc's countries to protect the mountains' nature and culture
together. A corridor drawn from below can speak that language
without becoming a reserve that fences people out. *(Claude's
proposal.)*

## 5. The Principles and Their Reasons

One list, each with its reason and what it rules out. The proposal
(§Principles), the starting prompt (§7) and the co-creator
agreement (§3) each carry a version of it, worded differently
(project review v0.1, D-6). *Claude's proposal: this list becomes
the source, and the others copy it at their next version.* The
principles are the project's; each *Why* is Claude's wording
unless it cites a decision.

**Honest offer.** Hosts offer only what they actually have, and
describe it as it is.
*Why:* a pilgrim who comes for a bed and finds a bed, for a meal
and finds the meal of that house, has met the place; one who comes
for a promise has met an advertisement. *Rules out:* inflating an
offer, borrowing a neighbour's craft, describing a place as it
should be rather than as it is.

**No performance.** The encounter is real or it is nothing.
*Why:* §3 — the moment a host performs, what is paid for is the
performance, and the way of life is lost more slowly but no less
surely. *Rules out:* staging tradition for visitors, costume,
demonstrations without a use.

**Organic growth.** The network grows from the inside, host by
host, not from a plan drawn above it.
*Why:* a route drawn in advance chooses villages for pilgrims; a
route that grows from hosts follows where people actually want to
receive. *Rules out:* assigning stages before hosts exist,
recruiting hosts to fill a planned route. *(T-5 asks how this
holds when the project began from outside.)*

**The Curator as memory.** People hold what no tool can. A tool
proposes; a person decides.
*Why:* who can receive whom, and when, in which family's season
and grief and harvest, is knowledge only a neighbour has. *Rules
out:* an algorithm answering a pilgrim, a booking engine, the
stage planner deciding anything.

**The pilgrim arrives.** A journey is undergone, not consumed.
*Why:* a pilgrim who arrives is changed by the place; a pilgrim
who consumes it changes the place. *Rules out:* itineraries sold
as products, checklists of sights.

**Off the beaten path.** Where people live, not where tourists are
sent.
*Why:* the resorts are already paid for; the villages are not.
*Rules out:* routing pilgrims to the places that already earn
from them.

**A pathway of relationships.** Person to person, person to land.
*Why:* §1 and §4 — the land is not scenery but a party to the
relationship.

**No one speaks for a community.** A host speaks for their own
place, a Curator for what they publish, the project for itself.
Support claimed in a community's name is a claim to be checked,
ours included. *(Michel's direction, October 9, 2026.)*
*Why:* §2 — the voice that is heard is usually the voice that
gains. *How it is built:* each host has their own account and
their own row, consents field by field and withdraws at will
(OI-P4); nothing about a place is public that its host did not
tick and a Curator did not choose.

**Not wilderness.** People and land are one and the same. What
needs protecting is the relationship, not a landscape emptied of
the people who made it. *(Michel's direction, October 9, 2026.)*
*Why:* §4. *Rules out:* framing a corridor as nature to be kept
from people, or supporting protection that bans the mowing and
grazing that made the meadows.

**Data stays with the people.** Each person decides what is shown
about them, can take it back, and can take it with them.
*Why:* a network that holds where people live, in wartime, holds
it in trust; trust that cannot be withdrawn is not trust. *How it
is built:* consent and publication as columns, withdrawal from
every map and export, full export (agreement §5).

**No calendar.** Availability beyond the season is a conversation
with the Curator, never a booking grid.
*Why:* a calendar is the first step to a booking service, and a
booking service turns a host into a supplier with slots to fill
(OI-P6).

**No ratings.** Hosts are not scored, ranked or reviewed in
public.
*Why:* a score teaches a host to perform for the score.
*(Agreement §3.)*

**Safety first, and the Curator holds it.** In wartime and in the
mountains, safety comes before any journey; shelter, border-zone
rules and the state of each stage are the Curator's to hold, not
the pilgrim's to find.
*Why:* a pilgrim from far away cannot know what a neighbour
knows, and published safety knowledge can be used against the
people it protects.

**A place is shown where its host puts it.** A published place, a
home included, is shown at the point entered, once a Curator has
published it (OI-P7, Michel's word, October 7, 2026). A host may
place their point at a nearby meeting place instead and tell
pilgrims the rest in person (agreement §7).
*Why — as the record shows it:* the host, not the project, decides
where they are to be found. *Michel's own reason for the decision
is to be written here; the record holds the decision and the risk
named before it ("the point is an address"), not the reason.*

## 6. What Success and Failure Look Like

*Claude's proposal, for Michel's word.* Neither is counted in
pilgrims.

**Signs that it works**
- Hosts are still hosting after three seasons, on their own terms,
  and say it was worth it.
- A young person learns a craft or a meadow's work from a host
  because the path brought them together.
- The Curators and stewards are people of the corridor, and choose
  the next corridor themselves.
- Money that pilgrims leave stays in the villages and is spent
  there.
- A community uses what the pathway shows to speak for its own
  land in a decision about it.

**Signs that it fails**
- Hosts begin to perform: costumes, staged meals, offers that grow
  to match what pilgrims ask for.
- Prices along the pathway rise faster than around it; houses on
  the map are bought by people who found them there.
- One village is saturated while others see no one.
- The Curator becomes an agent: pilgrims sold, hosts favoured, a
  fee taken.
- The project is described by how many pilgrims it moved.

## 7. Tensions Faced

Each tension is what bites, what already answers part of it, and
what is open. *The tensions were named by Claude on October 9;
Michel called them all relevant. Their answers are his.*

**T-1 — The map against "no performance."** A pin that says "A
workshop" invites its host to become what the pin says, and a map
is the tourist's gaze made permanent. *Already answers:* honest
offer; no calendar; no ratings; the Curator reads every place
before publishing; the click shows offers and terms, never a
score. *Open:* when a host begins to perform, who says so, and
what follows.

**T-2 — Paid, but not a transaction.** *Already answers:* four
forms of payment chosen by the host per offer — fixed, suggested,
exchange of work, ubec-pay or a community currency (OI-P8,
OI-P13); no figure on the map; no commission and no fee for
sending pilgrims (agreement §8). *Open:* what makes a
contribution different from a price, in words a host and a
pilgrim would both recognise; and why gift, the fifth form, was
not among the four — Michel's reason to be written. *Decided,*
October 9, 2026: payment passes directly from pilgrim to host
(OI-P13, A-3 closed).

**T-3 — Pilgrimage, to what?** There is no shrine, and the Hutsul
region has real places of religious pilgrimage. *Answered in part
by §4:* the pilgrimage is to the relationship between people and
land. *Decided,* Michel's word, October 10, 2026: the word is
kept, in the name Pilgrim of the Plai (*прочанин плаю*). *Open:*
whether the project says plainly that it borrows no one's faith.

**T-4 — The Curator's power.** The Curator publishes, suggests
journeys and holds safety knowledge: the memory of the path, and
also the one who decides who appears on it. *Already answers:* the
Curator never changes an entrant's answers, takes nothing from
hosts, and hands everything on when leaving; disputes go to the
Curator, then the lead, then a mediator (agreement §4, §8, §12).
*Decided,* October 9, 2026 (S-9): the first Curator is Michel,
named before the first hosts; the core body decides thereafter,
and later Curators are named by all co-creators. *Open:* what a
host passed over can do.

**T-5 — Organic growth that began from outside.** UBEC built the
map before there were hosts, and the project is led by one person.
*Already answers:* the hosts, stewards and Curators of the first
corridor are asked how the project is governed before Phase 2
opens (agreement §4). *Open:* what passes to the corridor and when
— for example, the corridor's Curators choosing the next corridor.

**T-6 — Inviting pilgrims into a country at war.** For:
solidarity, income where it is needed, witness. Against: risk to
guests, burden on hosts, a host's home made visible. *Already
answers:* safety held by the Curator; alerts first; anyone may
stop; pilgrims briefed (agreement §7). *Decided,* October 9, 2026
(OI-P12): a pilgrim from abroad prepares at home, and the Curator
repeats it to those who come through the Curator. *Open:* whether
to invite pilgrims from abroad at all while the war lasts.

**T-7 — Enough.** How many pilgrims a host, or a village, can
receive before the encounter turns into throughput. *Already
answers:* capacity per offer on every host point; the Curator
suggests only hosts with room; *decided,* October 9, 2026 (S-6),
no fixed ceiling, and the Curator keeps an overview of the
carrying capacity of each host, each stretch and the pathway.
*Open:* when the pathway refuses to grow.

**T-8 — The map as a buyer's guide.** In a rising market, a map of
old houses with elderly owners, each at its exact point, is also a
scouting list for buyers. This is new context bearing on OI-P7,
not a reopening of it. *Already answers:* a host may place their
point at a meeting place (agreement §7). *Decided,* October 9,
2026 (S-4): it is the host's own choice, and the Curator proposes
nothing.

**T-9 — Newcomers: learners and buyers.** The people who leave the
cities to learn the Hutsul way of life are the hope of the second
force (§2), and they also buy land. *Proposed line (Claude):* the
same as between a host and a performer — the relationship to the
place. One who comes to live and learn is part of the second
force; one who comes to own a view is part of the first. *Open:*
whether newcomers host on the same terms, and how the project
speaks of them.

**T-10 — Consent for the corridors.** A host agreed to be shown on
a map, not to become evidence for a protection corridor. The
agreement says the data is kept to put the network on the map, let
the Curator suggest journeys and let pilgrims find hosts, "for
nothing else" (agreement §5). So the corridors of §4 cannot draw
on hosts' places until the agreement says so, and a change in how
data is used needs each person's consent again (agreement §13).
*Open:* the wording, and a host's right to stay on the map while
declining that use.

**T-11 — Protection as pressure.** A designation can raise land
prices, and can ban the mowing and grazing that made the meadows.
"Not wilderness" guards the second. *Open, to be said outright:* a
protected landscape that only the wealthy can afford to live in
has failed.

**T-12 — The people inside the line.** A corridor traced from the
network crosses land where people live who never joined it.
*Open:* how "it speaks only for them" stays true when a corridor
is put before the C8, a ministry or a funder.

## 8. What We Do Not Yet Know

- How many of the corridor's houses and meadows are held by people
  over seventy, and what happens to them now. No source yet; Gate
  3's visits are the first chance to learn it.
- How many newcomers are settling in the corridor's villages, and
  why.
- What EU accession is already doing to land prices in the
  Ukrainian Carpathians, and what it did in the arc's other
  countries.
- Whether the Carpathian Eight will take up heritage and nature,
  and whether it will hear communities directly or through their
  officials.
- What the corridor's own people call the stewardship this
  document describes, in their own words. The Ukrainian text of
  this document should come from that, not only from translation.
